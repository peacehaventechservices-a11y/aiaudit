# Question bank

This skill does NOT run a long interview. Pain discovery happens in the recognition
check (research reflected back), not by grilling the owner. So there are only two
tiny moments where you ask questions: the **warm-up** (before research) and a light
**quantify** step (after they confirm pains). Keep both short. Ask one at a time.

## Warm-up — just enough to research them (2–3 questions)

The only goal is to learn who to research. Do not hunt for pain here.

- "What's your business called, and what do you do?"
- "Whereabouts are you based?" (name + location finds their Google profile)
- "Is it just you or a team — and roughly how many jobs/customers in a typical week?"
  (size + rough volume; the volume also feeds the running-cost estimate)
- Only if not already clear: "Have you got a website?" (don't gate on it)

Stop as soon as you have: business name, what they do, location, rough size/volume.
That's usually two or three questions. Then go and research.

## Quantify — a light touch, only on confirmed pains (1–2 questions)

After the recognition check, put numbers on the pains they ticked so the report and
quote have something real to size. Ask only about what matters most; don't quantify
everything.

- "Roughly how long does that take you a week — a couple of hours, half a day?"
- "How many [enquiries / quotes / jobs] a week, roughly?"
- "What's an average job worth to you?"
- "Out of the quotes you give, roughly how many turn into jobs?"
- "How often does that happen — every week, now and then?"

If they don't know a number, don't push — that item just carries lower quote
confidence and a range later. Never invent a figure to make the report look sharper.

## What you must have before writing the report
- The business name, what they do, location, rough size. (from warm-up)
- Which researched pains/opportunities they confirmed. (from recognition check)
- A couple of real numbers on the pains that matter. (from quantify)
- Rough volume, so running costs can be estimated.

That's the whole "interview" — deliberately tiny. Depth comes from research, not
from questioning.
