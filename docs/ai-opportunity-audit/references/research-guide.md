# Research guide

Two deep dives after the tiny warm-up (name, trade, location, size). Use web search
throughout. Research is the engine of this skill — it's what lets you name the
owner's pains for them and back every recommendation with real evidence.

## Business deep dive

Look at the real business, not just what the owner told you:

- **Website / listings:** what they offer, how they position, how they take
  bookings or enquiries. Note friction (e.g. "contact form only, no online booking").
- **Reviews (Google, Yelp, Trustpilot, Facebook, industry sites):** the single
  richest source. Positive reviews show what to protect; negative and lukewarm
  ones reveal recurring failures — slow replies, booking mix-ups, communication
  gaps — that often map directly to an AI fix. Look for patterns, not one-offs.
- **Social presence:** how much effort goes into content, and whether it's a
  time sink or a lead source.
- **Existing tool stack:** whatever's visible (booking widget, chat tool, CRM
  badge). Anything you recommend must bolt on *beside* these additively — never
  require changing, migrating, or replacing a tool they already run.

## Niche deep dive

Understand the vertical well enough to be credible, to build the pain list, and to
prove your recommendations work:

- **How the niche operates:** typical workflow, margins, seasonality, where peers
  commonly lose time or money. Search "[niche] common problems", "how [niche]
  businesses operate".
- **Evidence that a solution actually works:** for each idea you're considering,
  find proof — other businesses (ideally the same trade) using this kind of thing
  with a real result (time saved, money saved, more money). Search "[niche] AI
  tools", "AI for [niche]", "[task] automation results / case study". You're NOT
  finding a product to resell — existing tools are evidence and a price benchmark
  only. But if you can't find evidence the approach works, don't recommend it.
- **Keep it proven and simple.** Favour done-before, low-risk approaches. If the
  only version that delivers real value is a mature specialist product you'd have to
  rebuild from scratch (bleeding-edge R&D), that's not a recommendation — either
  find the simpler buildable version that genuinely helps, or drop it.

## Building the pain list (this is what Phase 3 rests on)

The recognition check shows the owner 3–5 likely pains and asks which ring true.
That list must be earned from research — a wrong guess breaks trust worse than
saying nothing. Build it deliberately:

- **Search the niche's real problems**, don't infer them: "[niche] biggest
  challenges", "[niche] owner problems / frustrations", "[niche] most
  time-consuming tasks", "why [niche] lose customers". Read what actual owners say
  in forums, Reddit, trade groups, and blog comments — their words are gold.
- **Mine the business's own reviews** where you can find them. A run of "took days
  to get back to me" or "never turned up for the quote" is a named pain you can
  reflect back almost verbatim — that's what produces "how did you know?".
- **Make each pain specific to the trade, not universal.** Test each candidate:
  would it fit almost any business? If yes, sharpen it or drop it. "Admin is a
  hassle" is filler; "quotes you drive out for that go silent" is recognisable.
- **Rank by how likely it is to land** and present the top 3–5. Quality over count.
- **Gauge your confidence.** Strong evidence → present the list plainly. Thin
  evidence (obscure niche) → present fewer, more tentatively, and lean on the
  "anything we missed?" question to fill gaps. Never dress up a hunch as insight.

A good gut-check before showing the list: would an owner in this exact trade nod
along, or shrug? Only show it if you're fairly sure they'd nod.

## Turning research into sizing

For each candidate opportunity, you want to be able to state:
- The pain or gap, ideally with the owner's own numbers (hours, frequency, £/$ value).
- What an existing tool would cost — as a *benchmark only*, to keep your build's
  price proportionate. You're not recommending the tool; you're pricing your build
  sensibly against what's out there.
- A defensible estimate of time saved or money made, and the reasoning behind it.

If you can't get to a concrete saving for a candidate, it probably doesn't belong
in the report. Better to drop it than to pad.
